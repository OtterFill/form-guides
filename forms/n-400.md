# N-400: fields and common mistakes

Form N-400, “Application for Naturalization”, is the form you file with USCIS to become a U.S. citizen. You fill it in yourself, usually as a lawful permanent resident of at least 5 years, or 3 years if you are married to and living with a U.S. citizen.

## At a glance

| | |
|---|---|
| Who receives it | USCIS, online through a USCIS account or on paper by mail to the address on the official page; a reduced fee or a fee waiver means filing on paper |
| Who fills it in | You, the applicant, usually 18 or older; you may file up to 90 days before you complete the 5-year or 3-year period |
| What happens next | Biometrics, an interview with the English and civics test, and if USCIS approves, the Oath of Allegiance at a ceremony: only then are you a citizen |
| Length | 14 pages in 16 parts, with 29 pages of instructions. This guide follows the edition dated 01/20/25 |
| Cost | Changes over time and is lower online than on paper: check the fee schedule (Form G-1055) linked from the official page |

## Who fills it in

You file Form N-400 if, in general, you are 18 or older and one of these applies:

- You have been a lawful permanent resident for at least 5 years (the general provision).
- You have been a lawful permanent resident for at least 3 years and, for those 3 years, married to and living with a spouse who has been a U.S. citizen all that time.
- You became a permanent resident as the spouse or child of a U.S. citizen who abused you, and apply under VAWA after 3 years.
- Your U.S. citizen spouse works abroad for a qualified employer, such as the U.S. Government, for at least 1 year after you file.
- You serve or have served in the U.S. armed forces: at least 1 year of honorable service, or service during a designated period of hostilities, where some requirements do not apply.

## Common mistakes

- **Filing too early.** You may file up to 90 days before you complete the period, not earlier, and every other requirement must already be met on the day you file. USCIS rejects early applications; use its Early Filing Calculator.
- **Writing the A-Number without 9 digits.** If your green card shows 7 or 8 digits, add zeros in front: A1234567 becomes 001234567. Write it at the top of every page.
- **Leaving answers blank.** Write “N/A” when a question does not apply and “None” when the answer to a number question is zero. Blanks slow the case down.
- **Leaving out trips or gaps.** List every trip that lasted longer than 24 hours, including short ones to nearby countries, and cover every month of the period in Parts 4 and 7, with “unemployed” where you had no job.
- **Not reporting an old or sealed arrest.** Part 9 asks about arrests, citations and charges anywhere and at any age, even if expunged or sealed. Not reporting one can lead to denial even when the offense itself would not have.
- **Counting marriages or children wrong.** Count annulled, religious and customary marriages, and the same person twice if you married twice. List all children under 18, including stepchildren and those living elsewhere.
- **Paying the wrong fee.** USCIS rejects a form with the wrong fee, the wrong payment method, or more than the fee. Check the fee schedule on the day you file.
- **Signing with a typed name, or not at all.** Sign Part 11 by hand. USCIS rejects an unsigned form; a photocopy or scan of a hand-signed form is accepted.
- **Using correction fluid or old pages.** Scanners can read through correction tape. Start a new form instead, and send all 14 pages of the same edition.
- **Not answering Selective Service correctly.** Men who lived in the United States between 18 and 26 answer 22.a “Yes” unless they were lawful nonimmigrants the whole time. Under 26, register before you apply.
