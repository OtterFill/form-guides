# I-864: fields and common mistakes

Form I-864, “Affidavit of Support Under Section 213A of the INA”, is the contract in which a sponsor promises the U.S. Government to support an immigrant financially and shows enough income or assets to do so. The sponsor fills it in: usually the U.S. citizen or permanent resident who filed the family petition, plus a joint sponsor if that income is not enough.

## At a glance

| | |
|---|---|
| Who fills it in | The sponsor, at least 18 and domiciled in the United States: the petitioner, and if needed up to 2 joint sponsors, each on their own form |
| Where it goes | The sponsor gives it to the immigrant, who files it with Form I-485 at USCIS or submits it in the immigrant visa case with the State Department’s National Visa Center |
| Income needed | At least 125% of the Federal Poverty Guidelines for your household size (100% for some active-duty sponsors). The amounts change every year: check Form I-864P on the USCIS site |
| Length | 12 pages in 11 parts, with 18 pages of instructions. This guide follows the edition dated 08/24/26, the only one USCIS accepts since October 1, 2026 |
| Cost | USCIS lists any fee on its fee schedule; in a visa case the State Department charges its own fee. Check both official pages |

## Who fills it in

You fill in Form I-864 as the sponsor if you are a U.S. citizen, U.S. national or lawful permanent resident, at least 18, domiciled in the United States, and one of these applies:

- You filed Form I-130 for a relative, Form I-129F for a fiancé(e), or Form I-600 or I-800 for an adopted child: you are the petitioning sponsor and must sign, even if a joint sponsor also signs.
- You filed Form I-140 for your spouse, parent, child, adult son or daughter, or sibling, or own at least 5% of the business that filed it.
- Your income is enough and the petitioner’s is not: you may sign as a joint sponsor, related or not to the immigrant.
- The petitioner died after the I-130 was approved, and you are a close relative or legal guardian of the immigrant: you may be the substitute sponsor.

## Common mistakes

- **Using an old edition.** Since October 1, 2026, USCIS accepts only the 08/24/26 edition. Send all 12 pages of the same edition; the date is at the bottom of each page.
- **Counting the household wrong.** Include yourself, your spouse wherever they live, unmarried children under 21, tax dependents, everyone sponsored on this form and those you still support from earlier affidavits. Count no one twice.
- **Writing joint income as your individual income.** Item 7 is only your own current income. Your spouse’s income counts only in Items 8 to 11 with a signed Form I-864A. In 16.a, by contrast, copy the total income of the return as filed.
- **Leaving out tax documents.** Send an IRS transcript or a copy of the most recent return with every W-2, 1099 and schedule; self-employed sponsors include Schedule C, D, E or F. Do not send state returns.
- **Thinking a joint sponsor can add the petitioner’s income.** A joint sponsor must meet the requirement alone for their own household plus the people they sponsor. The petitioner still signs an I-864 and sends a tax return.
- **Listing assets without proof or at gross value.** Use net value, after mortgages and liens, and send evidence of ownership and value. Assets must cover 5 times the shortfall (3 times for a citizen’s spouse or adult child).
- **Not signing by hand.** USCIS rejects an unsigned form, and a typed or stamped name is not a signature. Every sponsor and every I-864A household member signs their own form.
- **Leaving a credit freeze in place.** The new edition authorizes credit checks. If your credit is frozen and you do not lift the freeze when asked, the review may be delayed.
