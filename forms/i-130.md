# I-130: fields and common mistakes

Form I-130, “Petition for Alien Relative”, is the form a U.S. citizen or lawful permanent resident files with USCIS to prove a family relationship with a relative who wants to immigrate. You, the petitioner, fill it in about yourself and your relative, the beneficiary; it is the first step towards your relative’s green card, not the green card itself.

## At a glance

| | |
|---|---|
| Who files it | You, the petitioner: a U.S. citizen, a lawful permanent resident or a U.S. national; one petition for each relative |
| Who receives it | USCIS, online through a USCIS account or by mail to the lockbox listed on the official page for where you live |
| What it does | Proves the relationship and gives your relative a place in line; it gives no status. The green card comes later, through Form I-485 in the United States or an immigrant visa abroad |
| Length | 12 pages in 9 parts, with 11 pages of instructions; for a spouse, also Form I-130A. This guide follows the edition dated 04/01/24 |
| Cost | Changes over time: check the fee schedule (Form G-1055) linked from the official page. By mail, pay by card with Form G-1450 or from a U.S. bank account with Form G-1650 |

## Who fills it in

You file Form I-130 if you are a U.S. citizen, a lawful permanent resident or a U.S. national, and your relative is one of these:

- If you are a U.S. citizen: your spouse; your unmarried child under 21; your unmarried son or daughter 21 or older; your married son or daughter.
- If you are a U.S. citizen 21 or older: your parent, or your brother or sister.
- If you are a lawful permanent resident: your spouse; your unmarried child under 21; your unmarried son or daughter 21 or older.
- If you are a U.S. national born in American Samoa or Swains Island: the same relatives as a permanent resident; you tick “Lawful Permanent Resident” in Part 2, Item 36.
- Stepchildren and stepparents if the marriage that made the relationship took place before the child turned 18, and adopted children if the adoption took place before 16 and the 2-year custody and residence rules are met.

## Common mistakes

- **Leaving a required field blank.** USCIS rejects the petition if Part 1, your name, date of birth, mailing address or marital information, or your relative’s name, date of birth, physical address or marital information is missing. Write “N/A” or “None” elsewhere rather than leaving blanks.
- **Answering both 61 and 62, or neither.** Fill in exactly one: Item 61 if your relative is in the United States and will adjust status, Item 62 if they will apply at an embassy or consulate. Otherwise USCIS decides from the address you gave, and changing it after approval may need Form I-824.
- **Forgetting Form I-130A and the photos for a spouse.** A spouse petition needs Form I-130A filled in by your spouse, and 2 identical color passport-style photos of you and of your spouse if they are in the United States, taken within 30 days of filing.
- **Sending a marriage certificate and nothing more.** A certificate proves the marriage is legal, not that it is real. Add evidence of a shared life: a joint lease or property, combined finances, children’s birth certificates or sworn statements from people who know you.
- **Not proving that earlier marriages ended.** If you or your relative was married before, send the divorce decree, annulment or death certificate for each earlier marriage.
- **Filing one petition for the whole family.** Each relative needs a separate I-130, except derivatives in the preference categories. A U.S. citizen filing for a spouse also files one for each child they share, stepchildren included.
- **Mixing editions or using an old form.** All 12 pages must be from the same, current edition, with the edition date visible at the bottom. Check the edition on the official page before you print.
- **Paying the wrong fee or by check.** USCIS rejects a petition with the wrong fee. By mail, pay with Form G-1450 (card) or Form G-1650 (U.S. bank account), and check the fee schedule on the day you file.
- **Sending documents without a translation.** Every document in another language needs a full English translation with the translator’s signed certification that it is complete and accurate and that they are competent to translate.
